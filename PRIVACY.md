## Disclaimer / Privacy Policy (EU Compliance) <a id="disclaimer"></a>

HaGeZi DNS is a non-commercial, publicly accessible DNS resolver service operated privately for the public benefit in the EU. The operator acts as the data controller under the GDPR and as the provider of intermediary services under the DSA (see below).

- **Jurisdiction and encryption:** all DNS servers operate from EU data centers and fall under EU data protection laws, including the GDPR. User DNS traffic remains within EU jurisdiction. Encrypted protocols are supported and recommended wherever possible to maximize privacy.
- **Controller identity:** see [Service Operator](#operator) below for the identity and contact details of the party responsible for data processing, in accordance with Art. 13(1)(a)/14(1)(a) GDPR.
- **Purposes and legal basis:** the personal data processed consists of the source IP address of the connection and the domain name being looked up, both handled in transit only, for as long as answering the query requires. It is processed solely to provide DNS resolution, enforce rate limiting, and prevent abuse. Incoming connection IP addresses are additionally checked locally against downloaded third-party threat-intelligence blocklists (e.g., Spamhaus, AbuseIPDB) to block known malicious or abusive IPs at the firewall level. This matching happens entirely locally; no connection IP addresses are transmitted to these third parties. Independently of these lists, the operator also blocks source IPs observed directly attacking the service (e.g., flooding or attacking Do53), including a blanket block on Tor exit nodes due to persistent, ongoing attack traffic observed from that network. The legal basis for all of the above is Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR (legitimate interest in operating a secure and reliable service, and in network and information security, per Recital 49 GDPR). If you contact the operator, the message and its metadata are processed solely to handle your request, on the same legal basis. Processing is not based on consent, so there is no consent to withdraw under Art. 7(3) GDPR.
- **No cookies or tracking technologies:** this service operates at the DNS protocol level and does not use cookies, local storage, or similar tracking technologies, so no ePrivacy Directive consent requirements apply.
- **Data minimization and retention:** no personal data is retained, persisted, or shared beyond what is technically necessary to operate the service. Temporary query statistics are kept for a maximum of one hour, exclusively in memory. The totals and domain rankings among them carry no client identifier. The per-client query counters do: they are keyed to an IP address and are therefore treated as personal data throughout this document rather than as anonymous data. They are held in memory (RAM) only, for a maximum of one hour, serve solely to enforce rate limits, and are never stored linked to resolved or blocked domain data. Error logs ([SERVFAIL](README.md#glossary) events) are kept for 24 hours without client IP addresses. Correspondence sent to the operator is kept only for as long as needed to handle the request and any foreseeable follow-up, and is then deleted.
- **Retention of firewall block entries:** entries in the firewall blocklist are deliberately not held in memory alone, since a block that forgets itself provides no protection. The great majority of entries originate from third-party threat-intelligence lists and are replaced in full with every refresh of those lists, so an address stops being blocked as soon as the source list no longer carries it. A smaller number of entries are added manually after attacks observed directly against this service; these are kept for as long as the block remains necessary and are removed when it is not, including on request. A block entry consists of the address alone and is never linked to query data, resolved domains, or any other information. Because an entry holds nothing but an address, there is no way to identify or contact the person behind it, so the information duty for data obtained from a source other than the data subject is not discharged individually but through this document, on the basis of Art. 14(5)(b) GDPR (disproportionate effort). The sources of the third-party entries are named above, as required by Art. 14(2)(f) GDPR. If you believe your address is blocked in error, see [Data Subject Rights](#data-rights) below.
- **Status pages:** the `stats.txt` files linked in [README](README.md#server-health) are static text files, regenerated every 5 minutes from the DNS server's in-memory statistics and overwritten on each update. They contain aggregate totals and rankings only, with no client IP addresses and no per-client data. No web server access logs are kept for these files, and DNS query logging is not enabled on any endpoint of the service.
- **Recipients:** no personal data is disclosed to any third party for that party's own purposes. No data is sold, rented, or shared for advertising or analytics purposes.
- **Requests from authorities for data:** any legally binding order is checked for validity before it is acted upon, and data can only ever be disclosed to the extent that it exists. Given the retention described above, there are no query logs, no per-client history, and no stored association between a client and the domains it looked up, so no such data can be produced in answer to any request, whatever its legal basis. Orders directed at blocking a domain rather than at obtaining data are covered separately under [Content Moderation and DNS Filtering (DSA)](#dsa) below.
- **Processors (Art. 28 GDPR):** operating the service requires two infrastructure providers, each engaged under a data processing agreement and acting solely on the operator's instructions. **Hetzner Online GmbH** (Gunzenhausen, Germany) provides the server and network infrastructure in Falkenstein, Nuremberg, and Helsinki, and therefore necessarily handles connection IP addresses in transit at the network level. **Proton AG** (Geneva, Switzerland) provides the email service behind the `@hagezi.org` contact addresses listed in this document, and therefore processes the content and metadata of messages sent to the operator. Proton has no access to DNS traffic at all. Hetzner carries that traffic at the network level as an unavoidable part of providing the infrastructure, but receives no query data for its own purposes, and no query logs exist that could be handed over to anyone.
- **Third-party contact channels:** the Matrix support chat, direct Matrix messages, Signal, and the blocklist issue tracker on GitHub are run by independent third parties, not on the operator's behalf, and are therefore not processors. Using them is voluntary and subject to those providers' own terms and privacy practices. Note that the Matrix support chat and the GitHub issue tracker are public: anything posted there is visible to others and outside the operator's control. Email remains available as an alternative for every purpose described in this document.
- **International transfers:** DNS resolution takes place exclusively on EU-hosted infrastructure; no query data is transferred to or processed outside the European Economic Area (EEA). Email correspondence sent to the contact addresses above is processed in Switzerland by Proton AG. Switzerland is covered by an [adequacy decision](#glossary) of the European Commission, so this transfer requires no additional safeguards under Chapter V GDPR.
- **Mandatory provision of data:** processing your DNS query in transit is technically required to resolve domain names; without it, the service cannot function. No personal data beyond this technical minimum is requested or required.
- **Automated decision-making:** no profiling or automated decision-making with legal or similarly significant effects takes place.
- **Security of processing (Art. 32 GDPR):** security is achieved primarily through data minimization, so that little data exists to protect in the first place. Supporting measures include encrypted transport protocols, retention in memory wherever technically possible, with no per-client query data written to disk, a firewall restricted to the ports required for operation, regular patching of the operating system and DNS software, and no publicly exposed administrative interfaces. Measures are reviewed as the service and threat landscape evolve.
- **Personal data breaches (Art. 33/34 GDPR):** because virtually no personal data is stored, the realistic scope of any breach is limited to data in transit. Should a notifiable breach nonetheless occur, the operator will notify the competent supervisory authority without undue delay and within 72 hours where feasible, and will inform affected data subjects where the breach is likely to result in a high risk to their rights and freedoms.
- **Data Protection Officer:** given the limited scope, short retention periods, and purely technical nature of the processing, the operator does not consider a formal DPO appointment necessary under Art. 37 GDPR. This assessment is reconsidered whenever the scope or scale of the service changes materially. Privacy inquiries are handled directly by the controller (see below).
- **Minors:** this service is a general-purpose technical utility and is not directed at children. Art. 8 GDPR, which sets an age threshold of between 13 and 16 depending on the Member State, governs only consent-based processing in information society services offered directly to a child; it is therefore not engaged here, since this service does not rely on consent. No data of minors is knowingly collected or processed.
- **Reliability:** this is a best-effort, volunteer-provided service with no warranty, availability, or liability for interruptions or malfunctions. It is intended for private, lawful use only. Misuse, automated abuse, or attempts to circumvent restrictions may result in access being blocked.
- **Blocklist accuracy:** the operator does not guarantee the accuracy or completeness of the domain or IP blocklists used. False positives (legitimate domains being blocked, or legitimate users sharing an IP address with a flagged or abusive one) may occur. The operator accepts no liability for any issues resulting from incorrect blocking decisions.
- **Affiliation:** this service is not affiliated with any commercial provider, government body, or the DNS4EU consortium.
- **Acceptance:** use of the service constitutes acceptance of these terms.
- **Right to complain:** see [Data Subject Rights](#data-rights) below for the full list of rights, including the right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority.

### Content Moderation and DNS Filtering (DSA) <a id="dsa"></a>

As a DNS resolver, this service qualifies as a "mere conduit" intermediary service under the EU Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, "DSA"). Most of the obligations below apply regardless of the operator's size; only the annual transparency report (Art. 15) carries a small-scale exemption.

- **Terms and conditions on restrictions (Art. 14 DSA):** the policies, tools, and criteria used to restrict access to certain domains are described in the Filtering/Blocking section of [README](README.md#filtering): the two named public blocklists, the update cycle, and the reporting/redress channel. This information duty applies to all intermediary services regardless of size and has no small-enterprise exemption.
- **Notification of changes (Art. 14(2) DSA):** significant changes to these terms are reflected in the "Last updated" date at the top of this document and in the public version history of this repository.
- **Diligent and proportionate enforcement (Art. 14(4) DSA):** blocking decisions are applied in a diligent, objective, and proportionate manner with due regard to the fundamental rights to freedom of expression and access to information, and to the freedom and pluralism of the media.
- **Point of contact for authorities (Art. 11 DSA):** Member State authorities, the European Commission, and the European Board for Digital Services can reach the operator at [mail@hagezi.org](mailto:mail@hagezi.org) or via [Signal](https://signal.me/#eu/WlBfKuiT1S1GAGwDRpvIJErjM-C3IcjQUQ9HWLzeJKGKTfwlOGhEe7GQRSx05uX0) for more urgent matters. Communication is possible in German and English.
- **Point of contact for recipients of the service (Art. 12 DSA):** users can reach the operator directly and rapidly, not solely through automated tools, via [support@hagezi.org](mailto:support@hagezi.org), as described in the Getting Help section of [README](README.md#help). That address, [Matrix](https://matrix.to/#/@hagezi:tchncs.de), and [Signal](https://signal.me/#eu/WlBfKuiT1S1GAGwDRpvIJErjM-C3IcjQUQ9HWLzeJKGKTfwlOGhEe7GQRSx05uX0) all reach the operator privately. The [Matrix support chat](https://matrix.to/#/#hagezi-support:tchncs.de?via=tchncs.de) is available as a lower-barrier alternative, but it is a public room: anything posted there is visible to everyone present, so use one of the private channels for anything you would rather not share openly.
- **Legal representative (Art. 13 DSA):** not applicable. This requirement is limited to providers without an establishment in the EU; the operator is established within the EU.
- **No general monitoring (Art. 8 DSA):** filtering acts exclusively on the requested domain name in the DNS query itself. The service performs no deep packet inspection, no analysis of transmitted content, and no general monitoring of user traffic.
- **Basis for blocking, not government-ordered:** as of 2026-09-01, no domain has been blocked pursuant to a court order, government directive, or other legally mandated request (Art. 9/10 DSA orders), and no such request has been received. This statement is re-verified with every update to this document; the date above is the date it was last confirmed to be accurate. All current blocking decisions rely exclusively on the operator's own curation of the two named public HaGeZi blocklists. Should a legally mandated blocking request ever be received, the operator will independently verify its validity before acting, and this section will be updated to disclose that fact. Note that the DSA itself does not create an obligation to comply with such an order; any obligation would have to stem from applicable national or Union law.
- **Annual transparency report exemption (Art. 15(2) DSA):** the obligation to publish an annual content-moderation transparency report under Art. 15(1) DSA does not apply to providers that qualify as micro or small enterprises under Recommendation 2003/361/EC and are not very large online platforms; this exemption applies to this service. The operator nonetheless publishes the underlying blocklists and hourly statistics openly for transparency.
- **Voluntary use, no effect on open internet access:** using this DNS resolver is entirely optional. It does not alter, throttle, or restrict the underlying internet access service, and users remain free to switch to any other DNS resolver, an unfiltered resolver, or raw IP access at any time. As an application-layer, opt-in choice rather than a measure applied by an internet access provider, this service does not engage the Open Internet Regulation (EU) 2015/2120, which governs traffic management by internet access providers.

#### Copyright and Piracy-Related Blocking Requests <a id="copyright-piracy"></a>

Public DNS resolvers do not host or link to third-party content and, per recent German case law, generally do not play a "central role" in making copyright-infringing material accessible.

In *Sony Music v. Quad9*, the Oberlandesgericht Dresden (judgment of 6 December 2023, case no. 14 U 503/23) overturned the lower court rulings from Hamburg and Leipzig in full and held that a DNS resolver is liable neither as a perpetrator nor as a secondary infringer ("Störer") for third-party copyright violations, and cannot be validly ordered to block a domain on that basis alone. The court also held that the malware filtering offered by the resolver operator does not deprive the service of its neutral, intermediary character. That specific finding concerned security filtering; ad and tracker filtering goes beyond it and has not been tested in the same way.

The judgment does not rule blocking out altogether. In its closing section the court accepted that a resolver can in principle be required to block as a last resort under [§ 7(4) TMG](#glossary), where a rights holder has no other way to enforce its claim. Sony failed that test on the facts: it had made only single, unsuccessful attempts to serve the domain holder and the hosting providers, had not pursued the host provider through the courts, and had not approached the .to registry, the domain registrar, or the IP address registry at all, even though those parties, unlike a resolver, had a business relationship with the alleged infringer. The threshold is therefore demanding but not unreachable. Note also that the same provision gives a rights holder no claim for the pre-litigation and out-of-court costs of asserting and enforcing a blocking demand, unless the provider deliberately cooperated with the infringer.

Shortly before, the Oberlandesgericht Köln (judgment of 3 November 2023, case no. 6 U 149/22) reached the same conclusion for Cloudflare's public 1.1.1.1 resolver: no perpetrator liability, because the resolver plays no "central role" in making the content accessible and any other resolver would have answered the same query, and the mere-conduit privilege under [§ 8(1) TMG](#glossary) applies. The first-instance Landgericht Köln decision, which had extended liability to the resolver as well, was overturned on that point. The OLG did affirm liability for Cloudflare's separate content delivery network, which is a different kind of intermediary service and not comparable to a public resolver.

This is not an absolute guarantee: earlier proceedings in the same case (Landgericht Hamburg, 2021; Landgericht Leipzig, March 2023) initially ruled the opposite way, the Leipzig court going as far as treating the resolver operator as the perpetrator of the infringement itself, before both were overturned on appeal. The OLG Dresden did not admit a further appeal on points of law ([Revision](#glossary)), reasoning that it had applied settled principles of interpretation to an individual case. That left Sony Music one month from service of the judgment to lodge a [Nichtzulassungsbeschwerde](#glossary) with the Bundesgerichtshof. Whether it did so was never made public, and in the years since, no decision, withdrawal, or confirmation that the judgment has become final has been publicly reported. The legal position could therefore still evolve, including at the Bundesgerichtshof or the CJEU.

This service currently blocks no domain on copyright or piracy grounds and will update this section if that position ever changes.

### Data Subject Rights (Art. 15-22 GDPR) <a id="data-rights"></a>

As virtually no personal data is retained by this service, the practical scope of data subject rights under Articles 15-22 GDPR (including access, rectification, erasure, restriction, objection, and portability) is limited by the privacy-preserving technical design of the service: in most cases there is simply no stored data to access, correct, or erase. The main exception is a firewall block entry associated with your IP address, where these rights can be exercised in a meaningful way. All requests are reviewed and answered within the one-month period under Art. 12(3) GDPR, which may be extended by up to two further months for complex or numerous requests, in which case you will be told within the first month. For any privacy inquiries or requests regarding data processing, contact [privacy@hagezi.org](mailto:privacy@hagezi.org).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Your right to object (Art. 21 GDPR).** All processing described in this document rests on Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR (legitimate interests). You therefore have the right to object at any time, on grounds relating to your particular situation, to processing of personal data concerning you. Where you object, the processing will stop unless compelling legitimate grounds can be demonstrated that override your interests, rights, and freedoms, or unless the processing serves to establish, exercise, or defend legal claims.
>
> Two practical notes. Resolving a DNS query necessarily involves processing the source IP address while the query is in transit; an objection cannot be given effect while you continue to send queries to the resolver, so the effective remedy there is simply to use a different resolver, which you are free to do at any time. Where an objection concerns a firewall-level block of your IP address, it can be acted on directly and will be handled as described above.
>
> Send objections to [privacy@hagezi.org](mailto:privacy@hagezi.org). This notice is provided separately from the other information in this document in accordance with Art. 21(4) GDPR.

You also have the right to lodge a complaint at any time with a supervisory authority, in particular in the EU member state of your habitual residence, place of work, or place of the alleged infringement (Art. 77 GDPR), if you consider that the processing of your personal data infringes the GDPR.

## Glossary <a id="glossary"></a>

- **Adequacy decision:** a formal finding by the European Commission that a non-EU country provides a level of data protection essentially equivalent to the EU's, allowing personal data to be transferred there without additional safeguards.
- **Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR:** the "legitimate interest" legal basis, allowing processing of personal data when necessary for a legitimate purpose that is not overridden by the individual's rights.
- **Art. 7(3) GDPR:** the right to withdraw consent at any time where processing is based on consent; not relevant to this service, since it does not rely on consent as a legal basis.
- **Art. 8 DSA:** the rule that intermediary services have no general obligation to monitor the content they transmit or to actively search for illegal activity.
- **Art. 9/10 DSA:** the procedures for judicial or administrative authorities to order an intermediary service to act against specific illegal content or to hand over information.
- **Art. 11-13 DSA:** the requirements for intermediary services to designate reachable points of contact for authorities (Art. 11) and users (Art. 12), and, for non-EU providers, a legal representative in the EU (Art. 13).
- **Art. 12 GDPR:** governs how information and responses must be given to data subjects, including the one-month deadline for answering a rights request and the option to extend it by up to two further months for complex or numerous requests.
- **Art. 13/14 GDPR:** the duty to inform individuals about how their personal data is processed, whether the data is collected directly from them (Art. 13) or obtained from another source (Art. 14).
- **Art. 14/15 DSA:** the requirements to disclose content-restriction policies in terms and conditions (Art. 14) and to publish an annual transparency report on content moderation (Art. 15). The reporting duty applies to providers of intermediary services generally; Art. 15(2) exempts micro and small enterprises that are not very large online platforms.
- **Art. 37 GDPR:** defines when an organization must formally appoint a Data Protection Officer (DPO).
- **Art. 77 GDPR:** the right of any individual to lodge a complaint with a data protection supervisory authority.
- **Bundesgerichtshof (BGH):** Germany's highest ordinary court for civil and criminal matters.
- **CJEU (Court of Justice of the European Union):** the EU's highest court for interpreting EU law and reviewing questions referred by national courts.
- **Controller / Processor:** under the GDPR, the "controller" decides why and how personal data is processed; a "processor" (for example, a hosting provider) processes data only on the controller's instructions.
- **DNS4EU:** a European Commission-funded initiative providing alternative public DNS resolvers for European citizens and institutions, operated by a separate consortium; this service is independent of it.
- **DSA (Digital Services Act):** EU Regulation (EU) 2022/2065 setting due-diligence obligations for online intermediaries, including transparency and content-moderation rules.
- **EEA (European Economic Area):** the EU member states plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway, treated as a single area for data protection purposes.
- **ENISA (European Union Agency for Cybersecurity):** the EU agency that issues cybersecurity guidance and recommendations referenced by this service's security design.
- **ePrivacy Directive:** EU Directive 2002/58/EC, as amended, governing the confidentiality of electronic communications and consent requirements for cookies and similar tracking technologies; not triggered by this service, since it uses no such technologies.
- **European Board for Digital Services:** an EU advisory body of national Digital Services Coordinators that supports the consistent application of the DSA across Member States.
- **GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation):** EU Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the main EU law governing the processing of personal data.
- **Landgericht (LG):** a German regional court, typically the court of first instance for larger civil disputes.
- **Mere conduit:** a category of intermediary service (DSA Art. 4) that only transmits information without modifying it, such as internet access providers or DNS resolvers; it benefits from limited liability for third-party content.
- **Micro/small enterprise (EU definition):** a business category defined by Commission Recommendation 2003/361/EC (fewer than 50 employees and limited turnover/balance sheet), used by the DSA to exempt smaller providers from certain obligations.
- **Nichtzulassungsbeschwerde:** a complaint to a higher German court asking it to allow an appeal on points of law that the previous court refused to admit; if successful, the case proceeds to that court.
- **Oberlandesgericht (OLG):** a German higher regional court that hears appeals from Landgericht decisions.
- **Open Internet Regulation (EU) 2015/2120:** the EU net-neutrality rule restricting how internet access providers may manage or block traffic on their networks.
- **Recital 49 GDPR:** clarifies that processing personal data to the extent strictly necessary and proportionate for network and information security (e.g., preventing unauthorized access and denial-of-service attacks) is a legitimate interest of the relevant controller.
- **Revision:** an appeal to a higher German court limited to points of law rather than a fresh review of the facts; it must generally be admitted by the court whose decision is being challenged.
- **Störerhaftung:** a German legal concept of secondary liability for enabling a third party's legal violation, historically applied to internet intermediaries in copyright disputes.
- **Telemediengesetz (TMG), § 7(4):** the German provision under which a rights holder could demand that an access provider block the use of specific information to prevent a repeat infringement, available only where no other remedy was open to the rights holder, and expressly giving the rights holder no claim for the pre-litigation and out-of-court costs of asserting and enforcing that demand unless the provider had deliberately cooperated with the infringer. Like § 8(1), it ceased to apply with the TMG on 14 May 2024; it was carried over into § 7 of the Digitale-Dienste-Gesetz (DDG).
- **Telemediengesetz (TMG), § 8(1):** the German provision that implemented the EU mere-conduit liability privilege, exempting services that merely transmit or provide access to third-party information from liability for that information. The TMG ceased to apply on 14 May 2024 and was replaced by the Digitale-Dienste-Gesetz (DDG); the privilege itself now follows directly from Art. 4 DSA, to which § 7 DDG refers. The judgments cited above applied the TMG because it was still in force at the time, and the substance of the privilege is unchanged.
