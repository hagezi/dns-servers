## Disclaimer / privacy policy (EU compliance) <a id="disclaimer"></a>

HaGeZi DNS is a non-commercial, publicly accessible DNS resolver service, run privately for the public benefit in the EU. The operator is the data controller under the GDPR and the provider of an intermediary service under the DSA (see below).

- **Jurisdiction and encryption:** all DNS servers run in EU data centers and are subject to EU data protection law, including the GDPR. DNS traffic stays within EU jurisdiction. Encrypted protocols are supported and recommended wherever possible.
- **Controller identity:** the identity and contact details of the party responsible for the processing are given under [Service operator](#operator) below, in accordance with Art. 13(1)(a)/14(1)(a) GDPR.
- **Purposes and legal basis:** the personal data processed is the source IP address of the connection and the domain name being looked up. Both are handled in transit only, for as long as answering the query requires. They are processed solely to provide DNS resolution, enforce rate limits, and prevent abuse. Incoming connection IP addresses are also checked locally against downloaded third-party threat-intelligence blocklists (e.g., Spamhaus, AbuseIPDB), so that known malicious or abusive IPs can be blocked at the firewall. This matching happens entirely on the server; no connection IP addresses are sent to these third parties. Independently of these lists, the operator blocks source IPs seen attacking the service directly (e.g., flooding or attacking Do53), and blocks Tor exit nodes as a whole because of persistent, ongoing attack traffic from that network. The legal basis for all of the above is Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR (legitimate interest in running a secure and reliable service and in network and information security, see Recital 49 GDPR). If you contact the operator, your message and its metadata are processed solely to handle your request, on the same legal basis. No processing is based on consent, so there is no consent to withdraw under Art. 7(3) GDPR.
- **No cookies or tracking technologies:** the service works at the DNS protocol level and uses no cookies, local storage, or similar tracking technologies, so the consent requirements of the ePrivacy Directive do not apply.
- **Data minimization and retention:** no personal data is retained, persisted, or shared beyond what is technically necessary to run the service. Temporary query statistics are kept for at most one hour, in memory only. The totals and domain rankings in these statistics carry no client identifier. The per-client query counters do: they are keyed to an IP address and are therefore treated as personal data throughout this document, not as anonymous data. They are held in memory (RAM) only, for at most one hour, are used solely to enforce rate limits, and are never stored together with resolved or blocked domains. Error logs ([SERVFAIL](README.md#glossary) events) are kept for 24 hours without client IP addresses. Correspondence sent to the operator is kept only as long as needed to handle the request and any foreseeable follow-up, and is then deleted.
- **Retention of firewall block entries:** firewall block entries are deliberately not held in memory only, because a block that disappeared on every restart would not protect the service. Most entries come from third-party threat-intelligence lists and are replaced in full each time those lists are refreshed, so an address is no longer blocked once the source list stops carrying it. A smaller number of entries are added by hand after attacks seen directly against this service. These are kept as long as the block is needed and are removed when it is not, including on request. A block entry consists of the address alone and is never linked to query data, resolved domains, or any other information. Because an entry holds nothing but an address, the person behind it cannot be identified or contacted. The information duty for data not obtained from the data subject is therefore met through this document rather than individually, on the basis of Art. 14(5)(b) GDPR (disproportionate effort). The sources of the third-party entries are named above, as required by Art. 14(2)(f) GDPR. If you think your address is blocked by mistake, see [Data subject rights](#data-rights) below.
- **Status pages:** the `stats.txt` files linked in [README](README.md#server-health) are static text files. They are rebuilt every 5 minutes from the DNS server's in-memory statistics and overwritten on each update. They contain totals and rankings only, with no client IP addresses and no per-client data. No web server access logs are kept for these files, and DNS query logging is not enabled on any endpoint of the service.
- **Recipients:** no personal data is disclosed to any third party for that party's own purposes. No data is sold, rented, or shared for advertising or analytics.
- **Requests from authorities for data:** any legally binding order is checked for validity before it is acted upon, and data can only be disclosed to the extent that it exists. Given the retention described above, there are no query logs, no per-client history, and no stored link between a client and the domains it looked up, so no such data can be produced in answer to any request, whatever its legal basis. Orders to block a domain, as opposed to orders to hand over data, are covered under [Content moderation and DNS filtering (DSA)](#dsa) below.
- **Processors (Art. 28 GDPR):** two infrastructure providers are needed to run the service. Each is engaged under a data processing agreement and acts solely on the operator's instructions. **Hetzner Online GmbH** (Gunzenhausen, Germany) provides the servers and network in Falkenstein, Nuremberg, and Helsinki, and therefore handles connection IP addresses in transit at the network level. **Proton AG** (Geneva, Switzerland) provides the email service behind the `@hagezi.org` contact addresses in this document, and therefore processes the content and metadata of messages sent to the operator. Proton has no access to DNS traffic. Hetzner carries that traffic at the network level as an unavoidable part of providing the infrastructure, but receives no query data for its own purposes, and no query logs exist that could be handed over to anyone.
- **Third-party contact channels:** the Matrix support chat, direct Matrix messages, Signal, and the blocklist issue tracker on GitHub are run by independent third parties, not on the operator's behalf, and are therefore not processors. Using them is voluntary and subject to those providers' own terms and privacy practices. The Matrix support chat and the GitHub issue tracker are public: anything posted there is visible to others and outside the operator's control. Email can be used instead for every purpose described in this document.
- **International transfers:** DNS resolution takes place only on EU-hosted infrastructure; no query data is transferred to or processed outside the European Economic Area (EEA). Email sent to the contact addresses above is processed in Switzerland by Proton AG. Switzerland is covered by an [adequacy decision](#glossary) of the European Commission, so this transfer needs no additional safeguards under Chapter V GDPR.
- **Mandatory provision of data:** your DNS query has to be processed in transit for a domain name to be resolved; without it the service cannot work. No personal data beyond this technical minimum is requested or required.
- **Automated decision-making:** no profiling or automated decision-making with legal or similarly significant effects takes place.
- **Security of processing (Art. 32 GDPR):** the main safeguard is data minimization: data that is not kept cannot be exposed. In addition, the service uses encrypted transport protocols, keeps data in memory wherever technically possible and writes no per-client query data to disk, restricts the firewall to the ports required for operation, patches the operating system and DNS software regularly, and exposes no administrative interfaces publicly. These measures are reviewed as the service and the threat landscape change.
- **Personal data breaches (Art. 33/34 GDPR):** because almost no personal data is stored, a realistic breach could only affect data in transit. Should a notifiable breach nonetheless occur, the operator will notify the competent supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours, and will inform affected data subjects where the breach is likely to result in a high risk to their rights and freedoms.
- **Data Protection Officer:** given the limited scope, the short retention periods, and the purely technical nature of the processing, the operator does not consider a formal DPO appointment necessary under Art. 37 GDPR. This assessment is reviewed whenever the scope or scale of the service changes materially. Privacy inquiries are handled directly by the controller (see below).
- **Minors:** the service is a general-purpose technical utility and is not directed at children. Art. 8 GDPR, which sets an age threshold of between 13 and 16 depending on the Member State, only governs consent-based processing in information society services offered directly to a child. It does not apply here, because this service does not rely on consent. No data of minors is knowingly collected or processed.
- **Reliability:** this is a best-effort service run by a volunteer, with no warranty, no guaranteed availability, and no liability for interruptions or malfunctions. It is intended for private, lawful use only. Misuse, automated abuse, or attempts to get around restrictions can lead to access being blocked.
- **Blocklist accuracy:** the operator does not guarantee that the domain or IP blocklists are accurate or complete. False positives can occur, such as legitimate domains being blocked, or legitimate users sharing an IP address with a flagged or abusive one. The operator accepts no liability for problems caused by incorrect blocking.
- **Affiliation:** this service is not affiliated with any commercial provider, government body, or the DNS4EU consortium.
- **Acceptance:** using the service means accepting these terms.
- **Right to complain:** the full list of rights, including the right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority, is under [Data subject rights](#data-rights) below.

### Content moderation and DNS filtering (DSA) <a id="dsa"></a>

As a DNS resolver, this service is a "mere conduit" intermediary service under the EU Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, "DSA"). Most of the obligations below apply regardless of the operator's size; only the annual transparency report (Art. 15) has an exemption for small providers.

- **Terms and conditions on restrictions (Art. 14 DSA):** the policies, tools, and criteria used to restrict access to domains are described in the filtering section of [README](README.md#filtering): the two named public blocklists, the update cycle, and the channels for reports and redress. This information duty applies to all intermediary services, whatever their size, with no exemption for small enterprises.
- **Notification of changes (Art. 14(2) DSA):** significant changes to these terms are shown by the "Last updated" date at the top of this document and in the public version history of this repository.
- **Diligent and proportionate enforcement (Art. 14(4) DSA):** blocking decisions are applied diligently, objectively, and proportionately, with due regard to the fundamental rights to freedom of expression and access to information and to the freedom and pluralism of the media.
- **Point of contact for authorities (Art. 11 DSA):** Member State authorities, the European Commission, and the European Board for Digital Services can reach the operator at [mail@hagezi.org](mailto:mail@hagezi.org), or via [Signal](https://signal.me/#eu/WlBfKuiT1S1GAGwDRpvIJErjM-C3IcjQUQ9HWLzeJKGKTfwlOGhEe7GQRSx05uX0) for urgent matters. Communication is possible in German and English.
- **Point of contact for recipients of the service (Art. 12 DSA):** users can reach the operator directly and quickly, not only through automated tools, at [support@hagezi.org](mailto:support@hagezi.org), as described in the help section of [README](README.md#help). That address, [Matrix](https://matrix.to/#/@hagezi:tchncs.de), and [Signal](https://signal.me/#eu/WlBfKuiT1S1GAGwDRpvIJErjM-C3IcjQUQ9HWLzeJKGKTfwlOGhEe7GQRSx05uX0) all reach the operator privately. The [Matrix support chat](https://matrix.to/#/#hagezi-support:tchncs.de?via=tchncs.de) is an easier alternative, but it is a public room: anything posted there is visible to everyone in it, so use one of the private channels for anything you would rather not share openly.
- **Legal representative (Art. 13 DSA):** not applicable. The requirement only applies to providers without an establishment in the EU, and the operator is established in the EU.
- **No general monitoring (Art. 8 DSA):** filtering looks only at the domain name in the DNS query. The service performs no deep packet inspection, no analysis of transmitted content, and no general monitoring of user traffic.
- **Basis for blocking, not government-ordered:** as of 2026-11-25, no domain has been blocked because of a court order, government directive, or other legally mandated request (Art. 9/10 DSA orders), and no such request has been received. This statement is checked again with every update to this document; the date above is the date it was last confirmed. All current blocking decisions rest solely on the operator's own curation of the two named public HaGeZi blocklists. If a legally mandated blocking request is ever received, the operator will verify its validity independently before acting, and this section will be updated to disclose it. The DSA itself does not create an obligation to comply with such an order; any obligation would have to come from applicable national or Union law.
- **Annual transparency report exemption (Art. 15(2) DSA):** the obligation to publish an annual content-moderation transparency report under Art. 15(1) DSA does not apply to providers that qualify as micro or small enterprises under Recommendation 2003/361/EC and are not very large online platforms. This exemption applies to this service. The operator nonetheless publishes the blocklists and the hourly statistics openly.
- **Voluntary use, no effect on open internet access:** using this DNS resolver is optional. It does not alter, throttle, or restrict the underlying internet access, and users can switch to any other DNS resolver, an unfiltered resolver, or direct IP access at any time. Because it is an opt-in choice at the application layer and not a measure applied by an internet access provider, the service does not fall under the Open Internet Regulation (EU) 2015/2120, which governs traffic management by internet access providers.

#### Copyright and piracy-related blocking requests <a id="copyright-piracy"></a>

Public DNS resolvers do not host or link to third-party content. According to recent German case law, they generally do not play a "central role" in making copyright-infringing material accessible.

In *Sony Music v. Quad9*, the Oberlandesgericht Dresden (judgment of 6 December 2023, case no. 14 U 503/23) overturned the lower court rulings from Hamburg and Leipzig in full. It held that a DNS resolver is liable neither as a perpetrator nor as a secondary infringer ("Störer") for third-party copyright violations, and cannot validly be ordered to block a domain on that basis alone. The court also held that the malware filtering offered by the resolver operator does not take away the service's neutral, intermediary character. That finding concerned security filtering; ad and tracker filtering goes further and has not been tested in the same way.

The judgment does not rule out blocking altogether. In its closing section the court accepted that a resolver can in principle be required to block as a last resort under [§ 7(4) TMG](#glossary), where a rights holder has no other way to enforce its claim. Sony failed that test on the facts. It had made only single, unsuccessful attempts to serve the domain holder and the hosting providers, had not taken the host provider to court, and had not approached the .to registry, the domain registrar, or the IP address registry at all, even though those parties, unlike a resolver, had a business relationship with the alleged infringer. The threshold is high but can be met. The same provision also gives a rights holder no claim for the pre-litigation and out-of-court costs of asserting and enforcing a blocking demand, unless the provider deliberately cooperated with the infringer.

Shortly before, the Oberlandesgericht Köln (judgment of 3 November 2023, case no. 6 U 149/22) reached the same conclusion for Cloudflare's public 1.1.1.1 resolver. It found no perpetrator liability, because the resolver plays no "central role" in making the content accessible and any other resolver would have answered the same query, and it applied the mere-conduit privilege under [§ 8(1) TMG](#glossary). The first-instance decision of the Landgericht Köln, which had extended liability to the resolver as well, was overturned on that point. The OLG did affirm liability for Cloudflare's separate content delivery network, which is a different kind of intermediary service and not comparable to a public resolver.

This is not an absolute guarantee. Earlier proceedings in the same case (Landgericht Hamburg, 2021; Landgericht Leipzig, March 2023) first ruled the other way, with the Leipzig court going as far as treating the resolver operator as the perpetrator of the infringement itself, before both decisions were overturned on appeal. The OLG Dresden did not admit a further appeal on points of law ([Revision](#glossary)), reasoning that it had applied settled principles of interpretation to an individual case. Sony Music then had one month from service of the judgment to lodge a [Nichtzulassungsbeschwerde](#glossary) with the Bundesgerichtshof. Whether it did was never made public, and since then no decision, withdrawal, or confirmation that the judgment has become final has been publicly reported. The legal position could therefore still change, including at the Bundesgerichtshof or the CJEU.

There is also a geographic limit: these are German judgments, and they bind no court in another Member State. Elsewhere, courts have gone the other way. In May 2026 the [Tribunal judiciaire de Paris](#glossary) ordered three public resolvers (Quad9, Google, and Whalebone as the operator of DNS4EU) to block domains carrying unlicensed streams of WTA tennis matches, on an application by beIN Sports. Quad9 did not comply, and on 21 October 2026 the court imposed a penalty of €1,000 per day (case no. RG 26/11882). The next day Quad9 announced that it would stop serving France, citing about twenty further blocking actions pending against it there. It did so on 24 November 2026: since then, Quad9 refuses all queries it identifies as coming from France. An appeal is pending. Quad9 being established in Switzerland did not stop a French court from directing an order at it. The judgments above therefore describe what German courts have held about a resolver's liability for third-party content; they do not decide what a court in another Member State may order.

This service currently blocks no domain on copyright or piracy grounds and will update this section if that changes.

### Data subject rights (Art. 15-22 GDPR) <a id="data-rights"></a>

Because this service retains almost no personal data, the data subject rights under Articles 15-22 GDPR (including access, rectification, erasure, restriction, objection, and portability) have limited practical scope: in most cases there is no stored data to access, correct, or erase. The main exception is a firewall block entry for your IP address, where these rights can be exercised in a meaningful way. All requests are reviewed and answered within the one-month period under Art. 12(3) GDPR. For complex or numerous requests this period can be extended by up to two further months, in which case you will be told within the first month. For privacy inquiries or requests about data processing, contact [privacy@hagezi.org](mailto:privacy@hagezi.org).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Your right to object (Art. 21 GDPR).** All processing described in this document rests on Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR (legitimate interests). You therefore have the right to object at any time, on grounds relating to your particular situation, to the processing of personal data concerning you. If you object, the processing will stop unless compelling legitimate grounds can be demonstrated that override your interests, rights, and freedoms, or unless the processing serves to establish, exercise, or defend legal claims.
>
> Two practical notes. Resolving a DNS query necessarily involves processing the source IP address while the query is in transit. An objection cannot take effect while you keep sending queries to the resolver, so the effective remedy there is to use a different resolver, which you can do at any time. An objection to a firewall block of your IP address can be acted on directly and is handled as described above.
>
> Send objections to [privacy@hagezi.org](mailto:privacy@hagezi.org). This notice is presented separately from the other information in this document in accordance with Art. 21(4) GDPR.

You also have the right to lodge a complaint at any time with a supervisory authority, in particular in the EU member state of your habitual residence, place of work, or place of the alleged infringement (Art. 77 GDPR), if you consider that the processing of your personal data infringes the GDPR.

## Glossary <a id="glossary"></a>

- **Adequacy decision:** a formal finding by the European Commission that a non-EU country provides a level of data protection essentially equivalent to the EU's, so personal data can be transferred there without additional safeguards.
- **Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR:** the "legitimate interest" legal basis, which allows processing of personal data where it is necessary for a legitimate purpose and not overridden by the individual's rights.
- **Art. 7(3) GDPR:** the right to withdraw consent at any time where processing is based on consent. Not relevant here, since this service does not rely on consent.
- **Art. 8 DSA:** the rule that intermediary services have no general obligation to monitor the content they transmit or to actively look for illegal activity.
- **Art. 9/10 DSA:** the procedures by which judicial or administrative authorities can order an intermediary service to act against specific illegal content or to provide information.
- **Art. 11-13 DSA:** the requirements for intermediary services to designate reachable points of contact for authorities (Art. 11) and users (Art. 12) and, for non-EU providers, a legal representative in the EU (Art. 13).
- **Art. 12 GDPR:** governs how information and responses must be given to data subjects, including the one-month deadline for answering a rights request and the option to extend it by up to two further months for complex or numerous requests.
- **Art. 13/14 GDPR:** the duty to inform individuals about how their personal data is processed, whether it is collected from them directly (Art. 13) or obtained from another source (Art. 14).
- **Art. 14/15 DSA:** the requirements to disclose content-restriction policies in the terms and conditions (Art. 14) and to publish an annual transparency report on content moderation (Art. 15). The reporting duty applies to providers of intermediary services in general; Art. 15(2) exempts micro and small enterprises that are not very large online platforms.
- **Art. 37 GDPR:** defines when an organization must formally appoint a Data Protection Officer (DPO).
- **Art. 77 GDPR:** the right of any individual to lodge a complaint with a data protection supervisory authority.
- **Bundesgerichtshof (BGH):** Germany's highest ordinary court for civil and criminal matters.
- **CJEU (Court of Justice of the European Union):** the EU's highest court for interpreting EU law and answering questions referred by national courts.
- **Controller / Processor:** under the GDPR, the "controller" decides why and how personal data is processed; a "processor" (for example, a hosting provider) processes data only on the controller's instructions.
- **DNS4EU:** an initiative funded by the European Commission that provides alternative public DNS resolvers for European citizens and institutions, run by a separate consortium. This service is independent of it.
- **DSA (Digital Services Act):** Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, which sets due-diligence obligations for online intermediaries, including transparency and content-moderation rules.
- **EEA (European Economic Area):** the EU member states plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway, treated as one area for data protection purposes.
- **ENISA (European Union Agency for Cybersecurity):** the EU agency that issues the cybersecurity guidance and recommendations this service's security design refers to.
- **ePrivacy Directive:** Directive 2002/58/EC, as amended, which governs the confidentiality of electronic communications and the consent requirements for cookies and similar tracking technologies. It does not apply here, since the service uses no such technologies.
- **European Board for Digital Services:** an EU advisory body made up of the national Digital Services Coordinators, which supports consistent application of the DSA across Member States.
- **GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation):** Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the main EU law on the processing of personal data.
- **Landgericht (LG):** a German regional court, usually the court of first instance for larger civil disputes.
- **Mere conduit:** a category of intermediary service (Art. 4 DSA) that only transmits information without changing it, such as internet access providers or DNS resolvers. It benefits from limited liability for third-party content.
- **Micro/small enterprise (EU definition):** a business category defined by Commission Recommendation 2003/361/EC (fewer than 50 employees and limited turnover or balance sheet). The DSA uses it to exempt smaller providers from certain obligations.
- **Nichtzulassungsbeschwerde:** a complaint to a higher German court asking it to allow an appeal on points of law that the lower court refused to admit. If it succeeds, the case goes to that court.
- **Oberlandesgericht (OLG):** a German higher regional court that hears appeals against Landgericht decisions.
- **Open Internet Regulation (EU) 2015/2120:** the EU net-neutrality regulation that restricts how internet access providers may manage or block traffic on their networks.
- **Recital 49 GDPR:** states that processing personal data to the extent strictly necessary and proportionate for network and information security (e.g., preventing unauthorized access and denial-of-service attacks) is a legitimate interest of the controller concerned.
- **Revision:** an appeal to a higher German court limited to points of law, without a fresh review of the facts. It generally has to be admitted by the court whose decision is being challenged.
- **Störerhaftung:** a German legal concept of secondary liability for enabling a third party's legal violation, historically applied to internet intermediaries in copyright disputes.
- **Telemediengesetz (TMG), § 7(4):** the German provision under which a rights holder could demand that an access provider block the use of specific information to prevent a repeat infringement. It was available only where no other remedy was open to the rights holder, and it expressly gave the rights holder no claim for the pre-litigation and out-of-court costs of asserting and enforcing that demand, unless the provider had deliberately cooperated with the infringer. Like § 8(1), it ceased to apply together with the TMG on 14 May 2024; it was carried over into § 7 of the Digitale-Dienste-Gesetz (DDG).
- **Telemediengesetz (TMG), § 8(1):** the German provision that implemented the EU mere-conduit liability privilege, exempting services that only transmit or provide access to third-party information from liability for that information. The TMG ceased to apply on 14 May 2024 and was replaced by the Digitale-Dienste-Gesetz (DDG); the privilege itself now follows directly from Art. 4 DSA, to which § 7 DDG refers. The judgments cited above applied the TMG because it was still in force at the time, and the substance of the privilege is unchanged.
- **Tribunal judiciaire de Paris:** the French civil court of first instance in Paris, which among other things hears intellectual property disputes and applications to have intermediaries block access to specific content.
